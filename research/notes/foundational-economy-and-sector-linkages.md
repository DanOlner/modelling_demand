# The foundational economy and how sectors depend on each other

The larger project wants a holistic picture of how all sectors connect, including the foundational economy. This note covers:

- what the foundational economy is;
- why it fits badly with the GVA-per-worker lens;
- the main ideas about how tradable and local sectors depend on each other.

## 1. The foundational economy

**The Manifesto for the Foundational Economy** (Bentham, Bowman, de la Cuesta, Engelen, Ertürk, Folkman, Froud, Johal, Law, Leaver, Moran & Williams, CRESC Working Paper 131, November 2013):

- **Definition.** The goods and services "taken for granted" by all members of the population, and therefore spread across every territory.
- **Scope.** Both private and public. The provisional list is health, education, utilities, transport and distribution, banking, and food production, processing and retailing.
- **Size.** About 40% of the UK workforce, and nearly 30% of household spending.
- **The argument.** Economic policy focused on "leading edge" innovative businesses has overlooked these mundane sectors, even though they hold a very large share of jobs and businesses.
- **Material and providential.** The Collective's later work distinguishes the "material" foundational economy (pipes, cables and networks: utilities, transport, food supply) from the "providential" one (health, education, care, welfare). This distinction is from secondary summaries; I haven't checked it in their 2018 book. Confidence: medium.

**Liveability** (2023 onward) has three pillars:

1. residual income after essential costs;
2. access to foundational goods and services;
3. social infrastructure, such as parks, community hubs and public spaces.

By 2024 the Collective described a "liveability crisis", with the cost of living squeezing residual income. In Wales it has been taken up in policy debate; for example, the Foundational Economy Alliance gave evidence to the Senedd.

## 2. Why it fits badly with the GVA-per-worker lens

- **Prices.** Many foundational prices are regulated, publicly set or zero at the point of use. Webber & Huaccha's footnote 17 notes it may not be socially acceptable to raise the markup of S over C for public and merit goods (PDF p. 24).
- **Measured output.** Public services, about 20% of GDP, are measured by quantity and quality indicators, not market sales. For decades their output was assumed equal to their inputs (see `data-streetlight-effect.md`).
- **Scale.** Their Q is tied to the local population. In the paper's terms, the width of the rectangle is set by local demand, not by the firm's ambition.
- **Baumol's "cost disease"** (Baumol 1967).
  - The economy has a "progressive" sector with fast productivity growth and a "non-progressive" one, mostly labour-intensive services, with slow growth.
  - Wages across the economy follow the progressive sector, so the relative cost of health, education and care rises.
  - Aggregate productivity growth slows as these sectors take a larger share.
  - So places and economies with more foundational work show lower and slower-growing measured productivity, however well the services are run.
- **Unpaid work.** The providential foundational economy has an unpaid twin: household care and childcare, valued at £1.7 trillion in 2023 and outside GVA.
- **Implication** (mine). Judging places by workplace GVA per worker undervalues the foundational economy twice. It looks low-productivity by construction, and its contribution to living standards (residual income, access to services) is invisible in the measure.

## 3. How sectors depend on each other

**Economic base theory** (North 1955; Tiebout 1956):

- A local economy has "basic" sectors that bring money in by selling outside the area and "non-basic" sectors that serve local demand.
- Basic-sector income is re-spent locally, creating non-basic jobs through an export-base multiplier.
- In the North–Tiebout debate, Tiebout argued that private investment, government spending and local productivity also drive regional growth, not just exports.

**Local multipliers** (Moretti 2010, US cities):

- Each extra manufacturing job creates 1.6 jobs in local non-tradable sectors.
- Each extra skilled job in tradable sectors creates 2.5; an unskilled one about 1.
- High-tech industries have the largest multipliers.
- Extra jobs in one tradable sector have no significant effect on jobs in other tradable sectors.
- The mechanism is local demand. It is partly offset by higher local wages and house prices.

**The foundational economy reverses the emphasis.**

- The foundational sectors are the infrastructure that tradable sectors depend on: workers need care, schools, transport and housing.
- They are also where most jobs and household spending are.
- Economic base theory treats them as passive followers; the foundational view treats them as the base of everyday life.

**Leakage and local recirculation.** "Community wealth building", as in the Preston model (Preston City Council with the Centre for Local Economic Strategies, CLES), tries to keep spending by "anchor institutions" such as councils, colleges, universities and housing associations circulating locally.

- According to the council and CLES, locally retained spend rose from 5% to 18.2% in Preston, and from 39% to 79.2% across Lancashire, between 2012/13 and about five years later. That is £74m more in Preston and £200m more in Lancashire.
- About 4,000 more employees were reported to be receiving the Real Living Wage.
- These are the promoters' own figures; confidence is medium.

**In input–output terms** (see `flows-and-value-added.md`), these are backward linkages (purchases), induced effects (wage spending, Type II multipliers) and leakages (imports, profits paid to owners outside the area, commuters' spending at home).

## 4. What this gives the model (mine)

**A sector typology for a stylised economy:**

- tradable or "basic" sectors: manufacturing and assembly, IP and head-office services;
- material foundational: utilities, transport, food supply;
- providential foundational: health, education, care, often public;
- everyday "overlooked" sectors: retail, hospitality;
- housing;
- unpaid household work.

**Flows linking them.**

- Tradable sectors bring income into a place, and foundational sectors recirculate it.
- Leakages drain it: imports, profits to outside owners, and commuters spending where they live.

**Lenses.**

- Measured GVA per worker: foundational sectors look low.
- Liveability: residual income and access to services.
- Local multipliers and how much spending stays local.

**Experiments.**

- Austerity in providential services.
- Localising anchor-institution procurement.
- Attracting a high-tech plant (a Moretti-style multiplier).
- Investing in foundational provision.

Each can then be compared across the measured-productivity, income and liveability lenses.
