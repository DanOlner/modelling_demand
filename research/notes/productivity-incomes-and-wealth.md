# From productivity to people's incomes and wealth

GVA is produced at workplaces, while people live, earn, spend and save in places. The path from a firm's GVA to living standards runs through several splits, and any of them can move money between places and groups. Webber & Huaccha's (2024) rectangle stops at the first split, the bands within GVA (PDF pp. 12–13). This note follows the rest.

## 1. The chain of accounts

My summary, in national-accounts terms:

1. **GVA, by workplace.** GVA = compensation of employees + operating surplus and mixed income + taxes less subsidies on production. Depreciation sits inside operating surplus.
2. **Primary income of residents.** Residents receive the wages they earn wherever they work (commuting), plus property income (dividends, interest, rent), minus property income they pay out. Profits go to owners, who may be local, elsewhere in the UK or overseas.
3. **Disposable income (gross disposable household income, GDHI), by residence.** Primary income, minus taxes, plus benefits and other transfers.
4. **Residual income.** Disposable income minus the cost of essentials: housing, utilities, transport and food. This is the Foundational Economy Collective's measure.
5. **Wealth.** It changes with saving plus gains or losses on assets, especially housing and pensions.

Each step can differ by place and by household type. A model that wants to say something about "people's actual wealth" has to follow all five.

## 2. Evidence on each link

**Productivity to pay** (UK; Teichgraeber & Van Reenen, 2021–22):

- Mean hourly compensation grew in line with labour productivity from 1981 to 2019, so there is no "net decoupling".
- Median hourly wages fell about 25 percentage points behind productivity ("gross decoupling").
- About three fifths of that gap is rising inequality, with means outpacing medians. About a third is non-wage costs, especially employer pension contributions.
- Adjusted for self-employment and non-wage costs, the UK labour share fell about 3.5 points over four decades.

**Productivity to real wages, measured across whole supply chains** (Italy, 1999–2007; Garbellini & Wirkierman 2014): only 60% of productivity growth went to real wages.

**Workplace GVA vs residence income.**

- ONS says GVA per head is distorted by commuting and is not a productivity measure. It recommends GVA per hour or per job for productivity, and household income for living standards.
- GDHI per head in 2024:
  - UK £25,965;
  - London £36,487, the highest region, and North East £20,562, the lowest;
  - Westminster and City of London £84,292, the highest local area;
  - Leicester £16,786, the lowest.

**Rural places.** GVA per workforce job in majority-rural local authorities was 92% of the England (excluding London) average in 2023, and 82% where people live further from a major town (Defra). Many rural residents work in urban areas, so residents' incomes and workplace GVA differ.

**Ownership.**

- 58.8% of the value of UK quoted shares was owned overseas at the end of 2024; UK individuals owned 11.6% directly.
- A large share of the profit band of UK GVA therefore flows abroad or into pension and insurance funds, not to local residents.
- Quoted shares are only part of business ownership. Unquoted and family firms are not covered here.

**Wealth** (Wealth and Assets Survey, April 2020 to March 2022):

- Median household wealth was £293,700. The top tenth had £1,200,500 or more; the bottom tenth £16,500 or less.
- Median wealth was £489,800 in the South East and £179,900 in the North East.
- Property made up 40% of household wealth, private pensions 35%, financial wealth 14% and physical wealth 10%.
- Accreditation of the survey is suspended from this round on, so treat details with caution.
- My inference: much regional wealth difference runs through house and land prices, which are linked to local productivity and incomes only loosely and with long lags.

**Residual income and "liveability"** (Foundational Economy Collective):

- Living standards are better captured by income left after essentials than by GVA.
- Housing costs, which vary with tenure (outright owner, mortgage, private renter, social renter), drive much of the variation within and between English and Welsh regions.
- Their 2023 concept of liveability has three pillars: residual income, access to foundational goods and services, and social infrastructure.

**The paper's own claims.**

- Austerity and lower net immigration slow GVA growth, which "will stunt the rate of growth of workers' wages" (PDF p. 23).
- Higher wages raise Q through consumption (PDF p. 21).
- Both need the links above to be traced through to households.

## 3. What this gives the model (mine)

To get from productivity to people's wealth, the model needs:

- **a commuting matrix:** which residents work at which workplaces;
- **an ownership matrix:** who receives each firm's profits, including overseas owners and pension funds;
- **a simple tax and benefit system:** from primary to disposable income;
- **essential costs by place and tenure:** housing, utilities, transport, food, to give residual income;
- **saving and asset prices,** especially housing, for wealth.

It can then show four maps of the same economy that may diverge:

1. workplace GVA per job;
2. disposable income per resident;
3. residual income;
4. wealth.
