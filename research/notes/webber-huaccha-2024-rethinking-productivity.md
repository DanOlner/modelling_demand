# Webber & Huaccha (2024), "Rethinking productivity: the crucial role of demand"

This note covers three things: what the paper argues about demand, how it frames that argument in numbers, and what a model built on it would need. Material from other sources is in separate topic notes, listed under "Related notes" at the end.

Published figures in these notes are for orientation: plausible ranges, which effects are big or small, and where the evidence disagrees. They are not calibration targets, and the model is not meant to forecast.

Page numbers are PDF pages of `sources/Rethinking productivity the crucial role of demand.pdf`, as marked in its text copy. The journal's printed page number is the PDF page plus 53, so PDF p. 2 is journal p. 55.

## What the paper argues about demand

### The core claim

- **Supply-side bias.** Conventional analysis treats GVA per worker as one number on a single scale and explains it with supply-side factors: capital, labour and technology. It underplays demand (PDF p. 2).
- **Demand is built into GVA.** Firm-level GVA is revenue minus the market value of goods and services used up in production, and revenue is "strongly affected by demand" (PDF p. 3). So GVA, and GVA per worker, always contain a demand element, even though they are used as supply-side measures (PDF pp. 16, 20).
- **The 2D view shows where.** Splitting GVA into price, cost and quantity (the paper's "2D" view) shows that demand enters through two routes: the sale price S and the quantity sold Q (PDF pp. 13–16).
- **Strongest wording.** "GVA is the sum of potential productivity and demand excess/shortage" (PDF p. 16). Neither term is defined, and no equation is given.

### How demand gets into GVA, according to the paper

**Through quantity**

- **Two sources of demand.** Demand for a firm's product depends on (i) aggregate demand and (ii) demand specific to that firm's products. The paper calls this "an underappreciated issue in modern productivity analysis" (PDF p. 15).
- **Named drivers of Q:**
  - fashion;
  - persuasive advertising and "demand management" (Galbraith);
  - complements and substitutes;
  - feeling wealthier ("the opposite to austerity measures");
  - population growth through births, deaths and net immigration (PDF p. 16).
- **When Q falls.** If population and trade shrink, other things equal, Q falls and GVA productivity growth will be low or negative (PDF p. 16).
- **Demand density.** Citing Syverson (2004), demand density affects productivity separately from market size (PDF p. 16).

**Through price**

- **Prices move together.** Following Kalecki (1938/40), a firm's price depends on its elasticity of demand, which depends on the ratio of its price to the industry price. So a firm's price moves with the industry price (PDF p. 15).
- **Collective price agreements.** Industry-wide agreements to raise prices raise GVA. The example is OPEC/OPEC+ (PDF p. 15).
- **Elasticity sets the sign.** Whether a price rise increases GVA depends on elasticity. Industries with inelastic demand increase GVA when prices go up; those with elastic demand reduce it (PDF pp. 15–16).
- **Fees tied to asset prices.** Estate agents charge fees proportional to house prices. In a wealthy commuter area during a boom, S is large, C is small, and measured productivity is very high (PDF p. 15).

**Through firms shaping demand**

- **The tactics:**
  - status pricing (Veblen), such as functionally identical sunglasses differing only by brand;
  - psychological price points ($99.99);
  - fractional pricing ($137.31);
  - persuasive advertising, Galbraith's "want creation" (PDF p. 16).
- **Value added without efficiency.** These can raise value added through S without any gain in turning inputs into outputs (PDF p. 16).
- **Elasticities are uncertain.** The same tactics make estimates of price elasticity hard to trust (PDF p. 16).
- **Physical vs value measures.** Physical inputs and outputs are "likely to be loosely positively associated" with turnover and value added. Treating them as equivalent is "naïve and misleading for policy" (PDF p. 16).

### Market power and distribution

- **Market power widens the gap.** The gap between S and C widens through market power as well as through efficiency.
  - Example: a supermarket pays dairies $0.10 less per litre of milk and cuts its own milk price by $0.05. Its GVA rises whether or not Q rises (PDF p. 14).
  - Market power "tends not to be integrated into empirical productivity regression models" (PDF p. 14).
- **Kaleckian and Post Keynesian pricing** (PDF p. 14):
  - market concentration goes with higher markups;
  - trade associations and cartels regulate markups;
  - firms set prices and margins strategically, to grow and to manage competition, whatever the market structure (Melmies 2015; Lee 2013);
  - firms use variants of markup-on-cost pricing whatever the degree of competition (Lee 1998);
  - so the gap between C and S differs from firm to firm, and its spread across firms may differ over the business cycle.
- **Market power varies by place.** Local monopolies are the example, so national and industry-wide deflators may hide local conditions (PDF p. 14).
- **Supply chains across space.**
  - An urban yoghurt shop uses its market power to pay its urban-fringe manufacturer less. The manufacturer keeps its margin by paying the rural dairy less.
  - So "rural areas should have GVA figures that are lower than urban GVA figures even though the ratio of inputs to outputs … may be unchanged" (PDF pp. 14–15).
  - This is a prediction from the argument, not a finding. The paper presents no data.
- **Shrinkflation.** Keep S fixed and shrink the product, which lowers C. GVA rises even though customers get less for their money. The paper lists confectionery examples (PDF p. 15); see the worked examples below.
- **Competition for shares of GVA** (PDF p. 13):
  - Net profit, wages, depreciation and "other" costs all sit inside GVA and compete for shares of it.
  - Firms that need to pay dividends push to expand net profit. If GVA doesn't grow, profit can rise only if another band shrinks.
  - Unions defend employment or real wages, depending on their strategy.
  - Shares depend on managers' choice of production technique and on social norms.
- **How prices are anchored.** Prices are set through administered prices (Means 1936), average-cost pricing or markup pricing. The factors that set prices may differ from those that set output, wages or employment (PDF p. 12).

### Sticky prices and costs

- **Most prices are sticky.** A long list of literature, starting with Blinder (1991), says most firms keep prices fixed (PDF p. 15).
- **Firms watch competitors.** An earlier study by the first author (Webber et al. 2018) found some firms raise prices only after seeing competitors charge more, and some cut prices so as not to look greedy (PDF p. 15).
- **Recession behaviour.** In recessions consumers cut back on status-related spending, and solidarity may rise. So some firms may have held back both from raising S and from pushing down their suppliers' prices (C). The paper suggests this may show in the low inflation of the 2010s (PDF p. 21).
- **Contracts fix C too.** Nominal contracts between firms and suppliers make C sticky (PDF p. 21).

### Wages, policy and the productivity slowdown

- **Demand-cutting policies.** Policies that cut demand, such as austerity and reducing net immigration, "will inevitably reduce the rate of increase of GVA figures, holding all else equal" (PDF p. 4). The paper adds that this "will stunt the rate of growth of workers' wages" (PDF p. 23).
- **Demand-led growth.**
  - Productivity growth can come from wage rises. The labour share of GVA would first have to rise, at the expense of other bands (PDF p. 21, citing Lavoie & Stockhammer 2013).
  - Higher wages raise consumer demand and shift Q outward.
  - Higher wages that do not come at the cost of returns to other factors must go with higher S or higher Q (PDF p. 21).
  - Footnote 20 adds "This assumes there is inter-temporal redistribution", without explanation (PDF p. 25).
- **Three reasons GVA grows more slowly** (PDF p. 21):
  1. firms are reluctant or unable to raise S, because of implicit contracts or monopoly capitalism;
  2. firms are reluctant or unable to cut C, because of nominal contracts with suppliers;
  3. Q stagnates because aggregate demand grows slowly while job insecurity persists, perhaps because of austerity.
- **On the slowdown since 2008.** "If Q has been static since the GFC, then the flatlining of productivity suggests that the markup between C and S also has been relatively static on average across firms" (PDF p. 20). Research should find out whether productivity growth is slow because market power is rising, competition is falling, or Q is growing more slowly (PDF p. 20).
- **Stagnation arguments.**
  - Steindl (1945): in oligopolies, profits can be high enough for firms to cut investment, which slows technical progress and leads to stagnation. Excess capacity dampens investment (PDF pp. 20–21; footnote 19, PDF pp. 24–25).
  - Baran & Sweezy (1966): the economic surplus rises over time as big firms set high prices, maintain sales through advertising and compete by cutting costs. The paper says the 2D approach can be used to evaluate this (PDF p. 21).
- **Real wages and demand.** Falling real wages during and after the 2008 global financial crisis (GFC) reduced aggregate demand (OECD 2016). Underconsumption flattens output growth and causes recessions (Hobson 1910) (PDF p. 20).
- **Size of the slowdown.** ONS (2017) estimated that if the pre-2008 trend had continued, output per hour in 2015 would have been higher by 8% in Germany, 9% in the US, 14% in France and 18% in the UK (PDF p. 19).
  - Other explanations the paper lists: weak investment and intangibles; labour hoarding; bank lending and zombie firms; investment held back by low expected demand; secular stagnation; the global financial system; governance (PDF p. 19).
- **Policy conclusions.**
  - Stimulate domestic demand and overseas demand for domestic products, and recognise how austerity reduces demand (PDF p. 21).
  - Firms on the same isoGVA curve need different advice, so there is no one-size-fits-all productivity policy (PDF p. 18).

### Data

- **What researchers can see.** Researchers usually have only total revenue (SQ) and total intermediate costs (CQ), not S, C or Q. They need at least one of the three (PDF p. 22).
- **UK firm data.** UK firm-level data use approximate GVA (aGVA) from the Annual Business Survey rather than the national accounts. It covers fewer sectors, about three quarters of the economy (footnote 12, PDF p. 24).

### Capital-theory background, briefly

- **The argument.** The paper bases its rejection of aggregate production functions and total factor productivity (TFP) on the Cambridge capital controversies.
  - Capital measured in value depends on distribution, which is circular.
  - Reswitching and capital reversing break the one-way link between the interest rate and capital intensity, so factor substitution and marginal productivity theory fail (PDF pp. 5–6).
- **Consequences the paper draws.**
  - TFP treats productivity growth as coming only from technical progress, when most firms work below their technical frontier (PDF p. 8).
  - TFP ignores imperfect competition and non-constant returns to scale (PDF p. 9).
  - Badly measured sectors such as ICT and finance distort labour productivity figures (PDF p. 9).
- **What matters for a model.**
  - GVA per worker depends on managers' choice of technique, such as the capital–labour ratio. That choice varies across sectors and over time and depends only partly on technology (PDF p. 11).
  - Footnote 16 contrasts robotised and hand-built car plants with the same GVA but very different GVA per worker (PDF p. 24).
  - Adding firms up into one production function commits the fallacy of composition (PDF pp. 8, 13).

## How the paper frames it in numbers

### Notation and identities (the paper's)

- **Variables:**
  - S is the average sale price.
  - Q is the number of units sold.
  - C is the market value of goods and services used up or transformed in production, spread across the units sold, so it is intermediate cost per unit (PDF p. 11).
- **Areas in Figure 1** (PDF p. 11):
  - revenue (turnover) is the rectangle SAQO, $R = S \cdot Q$;
  - intermediate cost is the rectangle CBQO, $C \cdot Q$;
  - GVA is the difference, the rectangle SABC: $GVA = (S - C) \cdot Q$.
- **Per-worker productivity.** GVA per worker (GVApw), or GVA per hour, is $GVA / L$, where L is full-time-equivalent workers or hours (PDF p. 11). The paper notes that the right denominator depends on the bias it introduces when comparing firms.
- **Bands in Figures 2 and 3.** The GVA area is split into horizontal bands: net profit, labour, capital (depreciation) and "other". The figures label the whole area "Gross profit = GVA". The cost area below C is labelled "direct costs" (PDF pp. 12–13). Written out, $GVA = \Pi + W + D + O$ (net profit, wages, depreciation, other).
- **Where "other" sits.** The text says "other" holds extra expenses "such as utilities" (PDF p. 12). That puts utilities inside GVA; see "Points to check" below.
- **Ways GVA can rise.** GVA rises if C falls, S rises or Q rises. These can offset each other: for example, GVA can stay the same if Q rises while S falls (PDF pp. 13–14).

### Worked examples (each checked)

**1. A single firm (PDF p. 10).** Revenue is $50,000 and intermediate costs are $20,000, so GVA is $30,000. With three workers, GVA per worker is $10,000. The paper stresses that GVA "is not simply profit".

**2. Yoghurt supply chain (Figure 3, PDF pp. 12–13).** The text copy lacks the figure's numbers; these were read from the PDF.

| Stage | Sales | Bought-in direct cost | Other | Labour | Capital | Net profit | GVA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dairy farmer | 120 | 10 | 10 | 50 | 30 | 20 | 110 |
| Yoghurt producer | 270 | 120 (milk) | 20 | 50 | 60 | 20 | 150 |
| Yoghurt shop (supermarket in the text) | 570 | 270 (yoghurt) | 80 | 90 | 30 | 100 | 300 |
| Whole chain | 570 final sales | 10 | 110 | 190 | 120 | 140 | 560 |

- **Bands add up.** At each stage, sales = direct cost + GVA, and the bands add to GVA. Each stage's sales are the next stage's direct cost.
- **Chain total.** Chain GVA (560) = final sales (570) − the dairy's direct costs (10).
- **Ratios I derived** (dairy, producer, shop):
  - value-added share of sales: 92%, 56%, 53%;
  - labour share of GVA: 45%, 33%, 30%;
  - net-profit share of GVA: 18%, 13%, 33%;
  - S/C ratio: 12, 2.25, 2.11.
- **If "other" counted as intermediate cost.** The GVAs would be 100, 130 and 220, a chain total of 450 instead of 560.

**3. Supermarket squeezing the dairy (PDF p. 14).** The paper gives only the supermarket's side: its C falls $0.10 per litre and its S falls $0.05, so its margin per litre rises $0.05. The whole-chain arithmetic is mine:

- the dairy's S falls by $0.10 per litre, so if its own costs are unchanged, its margin falls by $0.10;
- consumers pay $0.05 less;
- at unchanged Q, nominal GVA across the chain falls by $0.05 per litre, and the supermarket's share rises.

**4. Shrinkflation (PDF p. 15).** These are the paper's weights, from Horton (2017); the percentage changes are mine.

| Product | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terry's Chocolate Orange | 175 g | 157 g | −10.3% |
| Maltesers (pack) | 121 g | 103 g | −14.9% |
| Quality Street selection box | 1 kg (2011) | 870 g (2012), then 780 g (2014) | −13.0%, then −10.3%; −22% overall |
| Twix | 58 g | 50 g | −13.8% |
| Snickers | 58 g | 48 g | −17.2% (the paper says "down 17%") |
| Dairy Milk | 49 g | 45 g | −8.2% |
| Toblerone | lighter, through wider gaps between the peaks | | no figures given |

If C falls in proportion to weight at an unchanged S, the margin $S - C$ rises. For example, with a 50% value-added share, a 10% weight cut raises the margin per unit by 10%. Whether official real productivity rises too depends on how price indices treat size changes; see the measurement note.

**5. Two firms with the same GVA (Figure 4, PDF p. 17).**

| Firm | S | C | Q | Margin S − C | S/C | Revenue | Intermediate cost | GVA | Value-added share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A ("Morgan"-style specialist) | 7 | 2 | 4,000 | 5 | 3.5 | 28,000 | 8,000 | 20,000 | 71% |
| B (automated mass producer) | 5 | 3 | 10,000 | 2 | 1.67 | 50,000 | 30,000 | 20,000 | 40% |

- **The firms differ in kind.** Firm A has a high price, low costs and low volume: a specialist product made by skilled workers, with more production stages in-house. Firm B has a small gap between C and S and a high volume.
- **Same GVA, different policy.** One-dimensional GVA would suggest the same policy for both. That would be wrong because "the responsiveness of demand to price changes would differ between the two goods" (PDF p. 17).
- **Different GVA per worker.** Footnote 16 adds that their GVA per worker could differ greatly with the number of workers, which depends on the choice of technique (PDF p. 24).

**6. The isoGVA curve (Figure 5, PDF p. 18).**

- **Axes.** The vertical axis is the ratio S/C; the horizontal axis is Q.
- **Points.** Firm A sits at (4,000, 3.5) and Firm B at (10,000, 1.67), joined by a falling, convex curve labelled isoGVA. Firms with the same GVA are said to lie on one isoGVA line.
- **What the paper says decides GVA.** Scale of production and the gap between S and C (consistent with Shapiro & Sawyer 2003).
- **Position on the curve:**
  - Firms up and to the left are more likely to sell status goods, where a high price signals prestige and low quantity signals exclusivity.
  - Firms down and to the right are more likely to sell necessities or homogeneous goods.
  - Footnote 17 suggests public-sector organisations may lie nearer B, because raising "the C to S mark-up" may not be socially acceptable for public and merit goods (PDF p. 24).
- **Policy.** Firms should "play to their strengths": some by expanding scale, others by widening the markup of S over C. Whether either is possible depends on their market conditions and on consumers' ability to exercise their demand (PDF p. 18).
- **Long tails.** The paper links this to the "long tails" in firm-level productivity distributions (Haldane 2018) (PDF p. 18).

### Decompositions that follow from the identity (my derivations, not in the paper)

These restate the paper's identity in forms a model can use directly.

- **Margin times quantity.** Let the margin per unit be $m = S - C$, which is value added per unit. Then $GVA = m \cdot Q$.
- **GVA per worker.** $GVA / L = m \cdot (Q / L)$: the margin per unit times physical output per worker. The paper's argument concerns the first factor, and its demand effects on Q; standard productivity thinking concerns the second. Both are hidden inside the one measured number.
- **Value-added share.** $v = (S - C)/S$ = GVA / revenue. The ratio on the paper's isoGVA axis is $S/C = 1/(1 - v)$, and the margin is $m = C\,(S/C - 1)$.
- **Growth rates.** Writing $\hat{x}$ for the proportional growth rate of $x$:
  - $\widehat{GVA} = \hat{Q} + \hat{m}$ and $\widehat{GVA/L} = \hat{m} + \hat{Q} - \hat{L}$;
  - the margin's growth is $\hat{m} = (S\hat{S} - C\hat{C})/(S - C) = \hat{S}/v - \hat{C}\,(1 - v)/v$.
- **Leverage.** Because of that last formula, a 1% price rise raises the margin by $1/v$ %, and a 1% fall in unit cost raises it by $(1 - v)/v$ %.
  - Firm B ($v = 0.4$): 2.5% and 1.5%.
  - Firm A ($v \approx 0.71$): 1.4% and 0.4%.
  - So firms with thin value-added shares, such as retailers and the mass producer, have GVA that is very sensitive to small changes in prices or bought-in costs. This sharpens the paper's point about supermarkets.
- **Labour share.** If $W = wL$ with wage rate $w$, the labour share of GVA is $w / (m \cdot Q/L)$, which is unit labour cost divided by the margin per unit. Raising wages without raising S or Q squeezes the other bands. This is the paper's claim on PDF p. 21 in algebra.
- **When a price rise raises GVA.** Take constant-elasticity demand $Q = A\,S^{-\eta}$ and hold C fixed. Then $d\ln GVA / d\ln S = 1/v - \eta$.
  - So GVA rises with price whenever $\eta < 1/v$. That is a wider range than "inelastic demand" ($\eta < 1$), which is how the paper puts it (PDF pp. 15–16).
  - The threshold is about 1.4 for Firm A and 2.5 for Firm B.
  - So firms with thin value-added shares can raise GVA by raising prices even when their demand is fairly elastic.

## Points to check before modelling (preliminary)

These are things I noticed while extracting. They are flagged for the next stage, which will interrogate the paper properly; this is not that critique.

1. **The isoGVA axis.**
   - Firms A and B have the same GVA in the margin form: $5 \times 4{,}000 = 2 \times 10{,}000 = 20{,}000$. So in (margin, Q) space they lie on one curve, $m = 20{,}000/Q$.
   - In the paper's (S/C, Q) space, the constant-GVA curve is $S/C = 1 + GVA/(C \cdot Q)$, which also depends on C.
   - With $C = 2$ fixed, Firm B would sit at $S/C = 2.0$, not 1.67. With $C = 3$ fixed, Firm A would sit at about 2.67, not 3.5.
   - So the drawn curve is only a single isoGVA curve if C is allowed to change along it. A model should probably use the margin S − C on the vertical axis, or show both views. Confidence: high; this is arithmetic on the paper's own numbers.
2. **"Other" costs and the "Gross profit" label.**
   - Figures 2 and 3 put "other" expenses, such as utilities, inside GVA. Official business statistics subtract purchases of "all goods and services", so purchased utilities belong in C (measurement note, section 1; confidence medium-high).
   - Moving them changes the yoghurt-chain GVAs a lot: 110/150/300 becomes 100/130/220.
   - The label "Gross profit = GVA" also differs from company-accounts usage, where gross profit is usually revenue minus cost of sales.
3. **Nominal vs real.**
   - The argument is in current prices. Official productivity is in constant prices, deflated by price indices.
   - The paper acknowledges this (footnote 18, PDF p. 24) and calls inflation adjustment "a veil of sophistication" that misses power struggles over prices (footnote 15, PDF p. 24).
   - Whether its examples (shrinkflation, a buyer squeezing suppliers, collective price rises) change real measured productivity depends on how the deflators work. This is the most important open issue for a model that wants to say something about official productivity statistics. See the measurement note.
4. **What data can and can't identify.**
   - S/C equals revenue divided by intermediate cost, and the value-added share equals GVA divided by revenue. Both are observable from standard firm data, even though S, C and Q separately aren't.
   - Standard data therefore tell you which isoGVA curve a firm is on and its S/C ratio, but not where along the curve it sits. That needs Q or a price.
5. **The step from GVA to GVA per worker is mostly missing.** The paper's analysis is almost entirely about GVA. The labour dimension (choice of technique, labour hoarding, physical output per worker) is where the supply side and the Kaldor–Verdoorn-type feedbacks would enter, and the paper leaves it to footnotes.
6. **Taxes on production.** GVA at basic prices also includes taxes less subsidies on production, such as business rates. The paper's bands leave these out. This is minor.
7. **"Potential productivity" and "demand excess/shortage"** (PDF p. 16) are not defined. One reading is an output-gap-style split of GVA into what supply capacity would allow and how far demand falls short of it. That reading is mine, not the paper's.

### Mechanisms the paper asserts, and candidate formal versions

| Paper's claim | Candidate formal version | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Aggregate demand drives Q (PDF pp. 15–16) | Firm Q = demand share × aggregate spending ÷ price. Spending from a Kaleckian or SIM-type closure | demand-dynamics note §1; ABM note §1 |
| Firm demand depends on its price relative to the industry (PDF p. 15) | Logit shares, replicator shares, or Kalecki's $S = m\,u + n\,\bar{S}$ | firm-pricing note §§1, 6 |
| Price rises raise GVA if demand is inelastic (PDF pp. 15–16) | With constant elasticity, GVA rises with S if $\eta < 1/v$ (see decompositions) | Wider than the paper's condition |
| Buyer power cuts C and raises the buyer's GVA (PDF p. 14) | Transfer price set by bargaining, stage markups, or a squeeze with partial pass-through | The sign depends on pass-through: firm-pricing note §2; supply-chains note §2 |
| Shrinkflation raises GVA (PDF p. 15) | C falls with pack content at constant S | Official price indices record it as a price rise: measurement note §§5–6 |
| S and C are sticky (PDF pp. 15, 21) | Price changes with a fixed probability per period, or only when a trigger is crossed; downward stickiness stronger | firm-pricing note §4 |
| Bands within GVA compete (PDF p. 13) | Wage-setting rule, dividend or profit target, chosen technique | ABM note §§2–3; demand-dynamics note §1 |
| Wage rises shift Q out (PDF p. 21) | Spending out of wages higher than out of profits; wage-led demand | demand-dynamics note §4 (method-dependent) |
| Firms held back on S and C in the recession (PDF p. 21) | Switch for whether markups rise or fall with the cycle | The evidence disagrees: firm-pricing note §4 |
| Steindl stagnation (PDF p. 20, fn 19) | Investment depends on capacity use and the profit rate; capital vintages carry productivity | demand-dynamics note §7; ABM note §2 |
| Choice of technique depends on distribution (PDF p. 11, fn 16) | Firms pick a capital–labour technique by comparing costs at current wages and profit rates; same GVA, different L | Not developed in sources here; K+S vintages are the nearest |
| Local monopolies and rural–urban chains (PDF pp. 14–15) | Locations on chain stages; firm territories | supply-chains note §4 |
| Austerity and net immigration lower GVA growth (PDF pp. 4, 16, 23) | Cut $G$, or change the number of spenders, in the demand closure; hysteresis feedback | demand-dynamics note §6 |
| Estate agents' fees follow house prices (PDF p. 15) | S as a fixed fraction of an outside asset price | A simple boom–bust scenario |
| Demand density matters (PDF p. 16) | Customers per territory; strength of selection | revenue-vs-physical note §5; supply-chains note §4 |
| GVA = potential + demand gap (PDF p. 16) | Measured GVA vs GVA at normal capacity use | demand-dynamics note §§1–2 |

### Scenarios the paper says matter

1. Austerity: lower government spending, lower Q, lower GVA growth, then slower wage growth (PDF pp. 4, 21, 23).
2. Reduced net immigration or population decline: fewer spenders, lower Q (PDF pp. 4, 16, 23).
3. Wage-led growth: a higher labour share, higher consumption, higher Q (PDF p. 21).
4. A buyer squeezing a supplier, with partial pass-through to consumers (PDF p. 14).
5. Shrinkflation (PDF p. 15).
6. Collective price rises, OPEC-style, with demand elasticity deciding the sign (PDF pp. 15–16).
7. An asset boom raising fee-based S (PDF p. 15).
8. Recession with firms holding back on prices and supplier squeezes (PDF p. 21).
9. Firm A vs Firm B under the same policy, scale vs margin (PDF pp. 17–18).
10. Rural–urban chain power (PDF pp. 14–15; Webber & Tiwasing 2025).
11. Steindl-type stagnation with rising markups and falling investment (PDF pp. 20–21).
12. The productivity puzzle as a split into S, C and Q contributions (PDF pp. 19–21).

### Levels the model could sit at

The levels form a ladder of increasing complexity, from a static identity explorer up to a full demand-driven economy with productivity dynamics. Each level adds one mechanism the paper discusses. The full ladder, with the existing models each level could borrow from, is in `agent-based-firm-demand-models.md`, section 8.

### What a browser model could show

- **The paper's rectangle**, with S, C and Q adjustable and the GVA bands visible. Two isoGVA panels side by side: margin vs Q, where the curves are consistent, and S/C vs Q, the paper's version. This shows the axis issue directly.
- **A cloud of simulated firms** on isoGVA axes, coloured by sector or location. It would show the long tail in GVA per worker, and that firms with the same GVA can differ in kind.
- **A supply chain** drawn as linked rectangles. Changing bargaining power moves GVA between stages and places while the chain total stays fixed, unless final prices or Q change.
- **Time paths** splitting growth in GVA per worker into margin, quantity and labour contributions, through a demand shock such as austerity.
- **Three measurement views** of the same economy: nominal GVA per worker, "official-style" deflated GVA per worker, and physical output per worker. This shows which of the paper's effects survive deflation (measurement note, section 6).
- **A phase diagram** over two parameters (Mark-0 style), for exploring the model space.
